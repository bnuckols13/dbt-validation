Validation

How to make someone feel heard without fixing, agreeing, or disappearing

Welcome

8 to 10 minAbout today

Most people think validation means telling someone they're right. It doesn't. Validation means communicating that a person's experience makes sense given their context. You can validate someone's pain without endorsing their conclusions. You can validate an emotion without acting on it.

Today we're learning validation as both a relationship skill and a self-regulation skill. Linehan identified six levels of validation, from simply paying attention to radical genuineness. We'll walk through each one, then practice identifying validating responses in real scenarios.

For many people in recovery, the harder version of this skill isn't validating others. It's validating yourself. The eating disorder functions as a self-invalidation machine: your hunger isn't real, your body is wrong, your emotions are too much. Self-validation is the direct counter to that pattern.

The skill

15 to 20 minLinehan's six levels of validation

Validation isn't one thing. Linehan identified six levels, from the simplest act of paying attention to the most demanding form of genuine human response. Each level builds on the one before it.

Clinical note

Validation does not mean agreement, approval, or permission. You can validate that someone feels terrified of weight gain without validating the conclusion that they should restrict. The emotion is real. The eating disorder's solution to that emotion is what we're working to change.

Level 1: Being present +

Give undivided, nonjudgmental attention. Put down the phone. Stop composing your response while the other person is still talking. Let them see that you are there.

This sounds easy. It is not. Most of what we call "listening" is actually "waiting to talk." Level 1 asks you to stay in the other person's experience without drifting into your own.

For self-validation, Level 1 means staying with your internal experience instead of numbing, distracting, or using behaviors to escape it. Noticing the urge without immediately acting.
Level 2: Accurate reflection +

Restate what you heard without interpretation. Reflect content and emotion back faithfully. This communicates "I actually heard you," not "I have an opinion about what you said."

Must be authentic, not formulaic. If it sounds like a therapy technique, it doesn't count. The person should feel heard, not processed.

Self-version: accurately naming what you feel. "I'm anxious right now" rather than "I feel fat," which is often a misattributed emotional state.
Level 3: Reading the unspoken +

Using behavioral cues, context, and what you know about the person to name what they haven't said yet. This requires willingness to be corrected. The other person remains the authority on their own experience.

Example: "I'm guessing that underneath the anger about the meal plan, there might be some real fear."

Self-version: recognizing what you haven't acknowledged. "I keep saying I'm angry at my dietitian, but I think I'm actually scared."
Level 4: Understanding in terms of history +

Communicating that the person's reactions make sense given their past experiences, their learning history, and their biology. This is where the biosocial model becomes directly therapeutic.

Example: "Given that you grew up in a household where emotions were dismissed and thinness was praised, it makes complete sense that you learned to manage distress through food restriction."

Self-version: "Of course I panic when I eat carbs. I spent years being told they were dangerous. My body is responding to learned fear, not to actual danger."
Level 5: Normalizing +

Acknowledging that anyone in this situation would likely feel the same way. This reduces the shame of feeling "crazy" or "too much," which is powerful for people who have been told their emotions are excessive.

Example: "Most people in early recovery feel terrified when their body changes. That fear is a normal part of this process, not evidence that something is wrong with you."

Self-version: "It is normal to struggle with this. Recovery is supposed to be hard. I'm not failing because I'm struggling."
Level 6: Radical genuineness +

Treating the person as an equal, not as fragile. Responding person-to-person rather than from a clinical role. This includes honest feedback, appropriate realness, and refusing to handle someone with kid gloves.

Example: "I believe you can do this, and I'm not going to pretend it isn't hard" rather than cheerful platitudes.

Self-version: being honest with yourself without self-attack or forced positivity. "This is really hard right now, and I'm doing it anyway. I don't have to pretend it's easy."
Facilitator note

Walk through each level, opening one accordion at a time. Ask the group for their own examples at Levels 1 through 3. Demonstrate Levels 4 through 6 yourself, because they require more clinical nuance. Read the self-validation asides aloud for each level.

The other side

10 to 12 minWhat invalidation looks like

Before you can validate well, you need to recognize what invalidation actually sounds like. Most invalidation isn't cruel. It's well-meaning. It comes from people who care about you and have no idea they're making things worse.

Linehan's biosocial theory says that emotional dysregulation develops when a biologically sensitive person grows up in an environment that persistently communicates: your internal experience is wrong. Over time, you internalize that message and become your own primary invalidator.

Clinical note

This section is not about blaming families. Many participants will feel protective of their caregivers. Normalize that: "You can recognize that your environment was invalidating and still love the people in it. Both things are true."

Common forms of invalidation

Dismissing +

"You're fine." "It's not that bad." "Don't worry about it." "You're overthinking this."

The message: your emotional response is disproportionate to the situation. The effect: you stop trusting your own internal signals. In eating disorder recovery, this sounds like "just eat" or "you look fine, stop worrying."

Fixing +

"Have you tried...?" "You should..." "What if you just..." "Here's what I would do."

Fixing skips past the emotional experience and jumps to solutions. It communicates: your pain is a problem to be solved, not an experience to be witnessed. The person stops sharing because every time they do, they get a to-do list instead of being heard.

Minimizing +

"At least you have..." "It could be worse." "Other people have it harder." "Be grateful for what you have."

Minimizing introduces a comparison that makes the person's pain feel illegitimate. It sets a threshold for suffering that your experience has to clear before it counts. In recovery: "At least you're getting help" or "There are people who are much sicker."

Cheerleading +

"You've got this!" "Stay positive!" "You're so strong!" "Everything happens for a reason."

Forced optimism communicates that the person's pain is uncomfortable for you, and you'd prefer they package it more neatly. It makes the suffering person responsible for managing the listener's discomfort. In recovery: "But you look so much healthier now!"

One-upping +

"I know exactly how you feel, when I was..." "That happened to me too, and I..."

Redirecting someone's pain into your own story communicates: your experience is a springboard for mine. The person learns that sharing means losing the floor. Empathy is not the same as identification.

Judging +

"Why would you feel that way?" "That doesn't make sense." "You shouldn't let that bother you."

Judging treats an emotional response as a character flaw rather than a signal. The word "should" is almost always invalidating when applied to emotions. Emotions don't follow logic. Telling someone they shouldn't feel something doesn't stop the feeling. It just adds shame on top of it.

Facilitator note

After walking through these, ask the group: "Which of these do you hear most often? Which one do you do most to yourself?" Most people will recognize multiple patterns. The self-directed version is where the real work begins.

Turning the skill inward

10 to 15 minSelf-validation in recovery

Most people in this room are better at validating others than validating themselves. If your best friend told you she was terrified about eating dinner, you would never say "that's stupid, just eat." But you say exactly that to yourself.

Self-validation means giving yourself the same response you would give someone you care about. In recovery, this is the direct counter to the eating disorder's voice, which functions as an internal invalidation machine.

The pattern

Invalidation turns the volume up on emotions, because the emotion has to get louder to be heard. Validation turns it down. This is counterintuitive. People think acknowledging pain makes it worse. The opposite is true.

Self-invalidation sounds like:

1
"I shouldn't feel this way."
Telling yourself your emotional response is wrong, excessive, or unjustified.
2
"Other people have it worse."
Comparing your pain to an imaginary threshold it has to clear before it counts.
3
"I'm being dramatic."
Labeling the emotion as a character flaw rather than a legitimate signal.
4
"I should be over this by now."
Imposing a timeline on healing that treats your own nervous system as an inconvenience.

Self-validation sounds like:

1
"This feeling is real, even if I don't like it."
Acknowledgment without judgment. The feeling exists. That's enough for now.
2
"Given what I've been through, this reaction makes sense."
Level 4 validation turned inward. Context explains the response. The response doesn't need fixing.
3
"I can feel this without obeying it."
Validation and action are separate. The feeling is valid. The eating disorder's solution to that feeling is what we're changing.
Facilitator note

Read each self-invalidation statement aloud and ask the group: "Raise your hand if you've heard this one inside your own head this week." Expect high recognition. Then read the corresponding self-validation reframe. Ask: "What shifts in your body when you hear the second version?"

Practice

15 to 20 minWhat would you say?

Each scenario is something someone in recovery might say. Four possible responses follow. One is validating. The others are fixing, invalidating, or dismissing. Read the scenario aloud, then ask the group which response they'd choose and why.

Disagreement is where the learning happens. When people choose different answers, explore what drew them to that response. The goal isn't to get the "right" answer. It's to feel the difference between responses in your body.

Scenario 1
"I followed my meal plan all week but I feel terrible about it. I keep thinking I don't actually need this much food."

B is validating. It names the internal conflict without trying to resolve it. A and D contain true information but skip past the person's emotional experience. C normalizes without actually engaging with what was said.

Scenario 2
"My mom keeps commenting on what I eat at dinner. I told her to stop and she said she's just worried about me."

C is validating. B is the trickiest, because it sounds compassionate. Ask the group: if you were the person who said this, how would you feel hearing B? That surfaces how invalidation often wears empathy's clothing.

Scenario 3
"I looked in the mirror this morning and just cried. I don't recognize myself anymore."

C is validating. A is the response most people give instinctively, and it does the most damage. It tells the person their grief is wrong because the outcome is "good." Ask the group: when have you received a response like A, and what did it do to your willingness to share again?

Scenario 4
"Everyone else in this group seems so much further along than me. I feel like I'm failing at recovery."

D is validating. This one generates the most disagreement because A and C both contain accurate information. Factual accuracy and emotional validation are different things. The question isn't whether the statement is true. The question is whether the person feels heard.

Scenario 5
"I had a really hard week and I used some old behaviors. I'm scared to even say it out loud."

C is validating. A minimizes the fear the person just named. B and D redirect away from the emotional experience toward action. C validates the act of sharing itself, which is what the person needs before any problem-solving can happen.

Scenario 6
"I know other people have it way worse than me. I feel guilty even taking up space in this group."

B is validating (Level 3/4). This scenario has a trick: A is also validating, but at Level 2. It's technically correct but can feel like a platitude. B reaches deeper by naming the pattern and connecting it to the person's history. Use this to show that validation has gradations.

Group process

10 to 12 minDiscussion

Question 1
The eating disorder is often described as a response to an invalidating environment. What does that idea bring up for you?
Let participants sit with the question before anyone responds. Watch for members who become protective of caregivers. Normalize ambivalence: "You can recognize that your environment was invalidating and still love the people in it."
Question 2
What's the difference between someone validating your feelings and someone agreeing with your eating disorder?
This targets a real clinical tension. Use it to teach the distinction between validating the emotion and validating the behavior. "Your pain is real" and "restricting is the right response to that pain" are not the same statement.
Question 3
What does your internal self-invalidator sound like? Whose voice does it remind you of?
After a few people share, ask the group to practice responding to one person's self-invalidation statement with genuine validation. This makes the skill real-time and relational.
Question 4
When someone tries to fix your problem instead of hearing it, what happens to your willingness to keep talking?
Most participants will recognize the shutting-down response. After discussion, connect to recovery: treatment teams and family often jump to fixing. How can participants ask for validation first?
Question 5
What would change in your daily life if you treated your own emotional responses as reasonable given your history?
This is the self-validation application question. Push for specifics. Not "I'd feel better" but "I'd stop arguing with myself about whether I'm allowed to be tired."
